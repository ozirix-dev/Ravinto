A broad report on the plant, nutrition, health and cultivation | 20 October 2026

Amaranth is an interesting group of plants to consider across nutrition, human health, cultivation and future food production. It is not simply a grain substitute: its seeds, leaves, young shoots, oil and natural pigments can all be used. Some varieties grow quickly and tolerate heat and dry conditions.

The amino-acid profile of the seeds, squalene in the oil and betalain pigments in red leaves are of particular interest. Amaranth can also be grown in Thailand, although leaf and grain production involve different practical choices.

Amaranth's main strength is the combination of useful traits in one plant: it is nutritious, versatile and relatively easy to grow in many environments. Marketing claims about its special compounds are stronger than the health benefits demonstrated in human studies.

## 2. What is amaranth? {#plant}

Amaranthus is a genus in the Amaranthaceae family, with dozens of species. It is related to plants such as quinoa, spinach and beet. Most amaranths are annual herbs, ranging from a few dozen centimetres to more than two metres tall, depending on variety and conditions.

Many species use C4 photosynthesis, which can support efficient growth in heat and strong sunlight. This makes them interesting for agriculture adapting to climate change.

### Important cultivated species

| Species | Main use |
| --- | --- |
| Amaranthus cruentus | Grain and leaves |
| Amaranthus hypochondriacus | Grain and flour |
| Amaranthus caudatus | Grain, ornamental use and leaves |
| Amaranthus tricolor | Leaf vegetable; red and green varieties |
| Amaranthus dubius | Leaf vegetable, especially in the tropics |
| Amaranthus viridis | Edible leaves; also grows wild |

Grain amaranths produce large flower heads with many small seeds. Leaf amaranths are grown for their green or red foliage.

### A brief history

Amaranth has been cultivated for thousands of years. Indigenous peoples of Central America, including the Aztecs, used it as an important food crop; it was also important in the Andes. It later reached Europe, where for a long time it was grown mainly as an ornamental plant.

Today amaranth is cultivated in Central and South America, India, China, Southeast Asia and several African countries. Seeds are especially important in the Americas, while leaves are widely used in parts of Asia and Africa.

## 3. Nutritional value {#nutrition}

It is important to distinguish dry seeds, cooked seeds and fresh leaves. Their values per 100 g differ substantially, especially because cooked seeds absorb water.

| Protein | 13.6 g | 3.8 g | 2.5 g |
| Fat | 7.0 g | 1.6 g | 0.3 g |
| Carbohydrate | 65.2 g | 18.7 g | 4.0 g |
| Fibre | 6.7 g | 2.1 g | — |
| Calcium | 159 mg | 47 mg | 215 mg |
| Iron | 7.6 mg | 2.1 mg | 2.3 mg |
| Magnesium | 248 mg | 65 mg | 55 mg |
| Potassium | 508 mg | 135 mg | 611 mg |
| Folate | 82 µg | 22 µg | 85 µg |

Rounded reference values from [USDA FoodData Central](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/). Values vary by variety, growing conditions and processing. The lower values per 100 g for cooked seed are largely due to absorbed water.

### 3.1. Protein

Dry amaranth seed commonly contains about 13–16% protein. Its lysine content is a particular strength: lysine is an essential amino acid that is relatively limited in some grains such as wheat. USDA reference data list about 0.75 g lysine per 100 g dry seed.

Amaranth can complement diets that rely heavily on grains for protein. Protein quality also depends on cooking, digestion and digestibility.

### 3.2. Minerals

Amaranth seeds are notable for magnesium and iron. Iron is needed for oxygen transport, but absorption of non-haem iron from plants varies. Phytates and oxalates in amaranth can reduce absorption of some minerals, so the amount listed in a food-composition table is not the same as the amount the body absorbs.

### 3.3. Leaves and vitamins

Amaranth leaves are a low-energy, nutrient-rich vegetable. USDA data for 100 g raw leaves include approximately 43.3 mg vitamin C, 1,140 µg vitamin K, 146 µg RAE vitamin A activity, 85 µg folate, 215 mg calcium and 611 mg potassium.

Vitamin C is reduced by cooking, and oxalates can limit absorption of some minerals. The value of the leaves comes from their combination of vitamins, minerals, plant pigments and low energy content.

## 4. Distinctive compounds {#compounds}

### 4.1. Squalene

Squalene is a natural hydrocarbon found in plants, animals and the human body. It is part of the biosynthetic pathway for cholesterol and other sterols and is also present in human skin sebum. It is of interest as a raw material in cosmetics, food and pharmaceuticals. Amaranth is one plant-based source.

A study of 104 amaranth genotypes reported an average seed oil content of about 5.0% (range 1.9–8.7%). Squalene averaged 4.2% of the oil and reached 7.3% in some samples. Using those averages, 100 g of seed would contain about 5 g oil and roughly 210 mg squalene. This is an illustrative calculation, not a fixed value for every variety. Squalene is concentrated in the oil, not in the whole seed mass; leaves contain much less.

Research considers squalene's chemical properties, oxidation, skin function and pharmaceutical uses. Its biological role does not show that eating amaranth prevents cancer, extends life or treats heart disease. Squalene is not an essential nutrient that people must obtain from amaranth.

### 4.2. Betalains

The vivid colour of red amaranth is largely due to betalains, especially red-purple betacyanins. One characteristic pigment is amaranthin. Betalains are chemically different from blueberry anthocyanins, even though both groups can produce red or purple colours.

Laboratory studies have found antioxidant properties, but human evidence does not establish that amaranth pigments meaningfully prevent chronic disease. Pigment levels vary greatly among varieties.

### 4.3. Other compounds

| Compound | Why it is studied |
| --- | --- |
| Squalene | Sterol-biosynthesis intermediate and industrial ingredient |
| Tocopherols and tocotrienols | Forms of vitamin E |
| Betacyanins | Natural pigments studied for antioxidant properties |
| Carotenoids | Plant pigments, including vitamin A precursors |
| Phenolic compounds | Plant defence and antioxidant systems |
| Bioactive peptides | Laboratory research, including enzyme effects |
| Dietary fibre | Supports digestion and satiety |

Environmental stress can change levels of protective compounds in plants. A higher concentration does not automatically mean a safer or healthier food.

## 5. What do we know about health effects? {#evidence}

Nutrient composition, laboratory findings and demonstrated health benefits in people are different kinds of evidence. Results also depend on whether a study uses leaves, whole seeds, oil or a concentrated extract.

| Claim | Evidence assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Amaranth provides protein and minerals | Well-supported composition data |
| It lowers cholesterol | Human findings conflict; a reliable benefit has not been established |
| It lowers blood glucose | Some promising but preliminary observations; no proven treatment effect |
| It prevents cancer or heart disease | Not established in humans |

### Cholesterol: human findings conflict

In a 2010 randomized, double-blind trial, 22 people with moderate hypercholesterolaemia consumed 50 g per day of a defatted amaranth snack or a corn snack for two months. Changes in total cholesterol, LDL and triglycerides did not differ significantly between groups; HDL fell more in the amaranth group (p = 0.03). The trial did not show an LDL reduction. Chávez-Jáuregui et al., *Effects of defatted amaranth (Amaranthus caudatus L.) snacks on lipid metabolism of patients with moderate hypercholesterolemia*, Ciência e Tecnologia de Alimentos 30(4), 1007–1010 (2010), DOI [10.1590/S0101-20612010000400026](https://doi.org/10.1590/S0101-20612010000400026).

In a 2019 randomized, double-blind crossover trial, 44 overweight or obese participants took 20 mL of amaranth oil and rapeseed oil in separate three-week periods, with a washout period between them. Changes in total and LDL cholesterol were less favourable during the amaranth-oil period than during the rapeseed-oil period (p = 0.002 for both); other analyzed outcomes, including HDL and triglycerides, did not differ significantly between groups. The trial does not support amaranth oil as a cholesterol-lowering product. Dus-Zuchowska et al., *Amaranth Oil Increases Total and LDL Cholesterol Levels without Influencing Early Markers of Atherosclerosis in an Overweight and Obese Population*, Nutrients (2019), DOI [10.3390/nu11123069](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11123069), PMID [31888234](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31888234/). A 2021 paper analyzed the same participants and is not an independent trial [DOI 10.3390/nu13082743](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13082743).

A preliminary observational report published in 2026 found that total cholesterol (212.16 to 197.03 mg/dL) and LDL (133.4 to 123.86 mg/dL) fell over three months among people taking an Amaranthus caudatus seed extract, but neither change was statistically significant. Sixty people enrolled and three-month outcome data were available for 37; sample sizes varied by analysis. There was no control or placebo group, participants received dietary advice, and NatIng Italia, the extract supplier, funded the study and participated in the research, analysis and interpretation. These before-and-after observations do not establish that the extract caused the changes. Mattiello et al., *Preliminary evaluation of an extract of Amaranthus caudatus as a novel nutraceutical: effects on cholesterol and glucose absorption*, Frontiers in Nutrition (12 Jan 2026), DOI [10.3389/fnut.2025.1740097](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1740097).

### Blood glucose: the product and preparation matter

A small study divided 90 postmenopausal women into three groups. One group received 9 g of dried amaranth leaf powder daily for three months; fasting glucose was reported to fall by 10.4%. This is an interesting finding in a specific group, but one small study does not establish that the powder treats diabetes or that the result applies to others. [Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26396347/)

Preparation may also affect glucose response. In a 2015 study of 20 healthy volunteers, the adjusted glycaemic index was 45.9 for an amaranth breakfast cereal, 74.1 for the comparison cereal, and 105.2 for popped amaranth alone. These values apply to the tested products and small study sample, not to amaranth in general. Schneider, Heinemann and Hahn, *Comparison of Glycemic Index and Satiety of Cereals containing Amaranth*, Journal of Human Nutrition and Food Science 3(5), 1074 (2015), DOI 10.47739/2333-6706/1074 ([full text](https://www.jscimedcentral.com/journal-article-info/Journal-of-Human-Nutrition-and-Food-Science/Comparison-of-Glycemic--Index-and-Satiety-of-Cereals--containing-Amaranth-8443)).

In the same 2026 report, mean HbA1c fell from 5.8 ± 0.4% to 5.3 ± 0.6%; the authors reported statistically significant changes in HbA1c, post-meal glucose and BMI. The fasting-glucose change was not significant. The uncontrolled design and lack of a comparison group prevent causal conclusions; the study evaluated an extract, not ordinary amaranth foods. [Study report](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1740097).

The same report found reduced glucose and cholesterol transport in a gut model. This is a laboratory result, not an equivalent percentage reduction in a person's blood glucose or cholesterol.

### Summary

Human studies have not reliably shown that amaranth or amaranth oil lowers cholesterol. Blood-glucose findings are preliminary and vary by product: leaf powder, seed extract, grain mixes and popped seed are not the same intervention. Amaranth can be part of a varied diet, but should not be considered medicine or a replacement for prescribed treatment.

### Population observations across regions and longevity

Amaranth use has been studied in several regions, but these studies do not show that eating amaranth extends life.

- **Kenya and Tanzania:** In 20 focus group discussions and 20 key-informant interviews, amaranth was named among the three preferred local leafy vegetables in about 90% of focus groups. This describes the preferences of discussion groups, not the percentage of the population consuming it or any health effect. Researchers reported that it was often eaten because it was affordable and available. [Nyonje et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9233608/)
- **Mexico:** In a survey of 70 amaranth farmers in Tochimilco, Puebla, 77% of households said they did not consume amaranth. Growing a crop locally does not necessarily mean that residents eat it. [Corona-González et al., 2019](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S2395-91692019000200123&script=sci_abstract&tlng=en) The San Luis Potosí health authority reported that 3,769 children recovered from malnutrition during 2013 in a programme that included amaranth flour. The report had no comparison group, so the outcome cannot be attributed to amaranth alone. [Mexican health authority, 2014](https://www.gob.mx/salud/prensa/control-nutricional-a-menores-de-5-anos)
- **A small Mexican pilot:** In a non-randomized study, nine children with low height-for-age received 4 g of popped amaranth daily for three months. Changes in gut microbiota, including the relative abundance of the Akkermansiaceae family, were observed. This small microbiome study does not demonstrate a clinical benefit or longer life. [2023 study](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/12/14/2760)
- **Ethiopia:** In a six-month study of anemic children aged 2–5, bread containing amaranth and chickpea reduced anemia compared with maize bread. The product combined ingredients and was specially processed, so the effect cannot be attributed to amaranth alone. The result applies to that food intervention and age group, not to longevity. [Orsango et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986748/)
- **Kenya:** In a 16-week study of preschool children, porridge made with amaranth and maize did not significantly improve haemoglobin or iron status. Results therefore vary by product and study design. [Study on PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22810982/)
- **United States:** Large cohorts have associated higher overall whole-grain intake with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. These studies did not isolate amaranth, so they cannot show that amaranth itself extends life. [Wu et al., 2015](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2087877)

**Conclusion on longevity:** There is no reliable long-term population study showing that high amaranth consumption extends life or reduces mortality. Regional food-use studies describe traditions and availability, not health effects by themselves. Overall diet, income, food security and access to healthcare also shape population health.

## 6. Safety and possible concerns {#safety}

### Oxalates

Seeds and leaves contain oxalates, and some leaf varieties can have high levels. Oxalates can bind minerals and may be relevant to people prone to oxalate kidney stones. In a study of 30 seed-amaranth accessions, oxalate averaged 229 mg per 100 g of seed; most was insoluble. This does not directly establish the same absorption risk as soluble oxalate.

Boiling high-oxalate leaves and discarding the cooking water can reduce oxalate, but also removes some water-soluble nutrients.

### Nitrates

Leaf amaranth can accumulate nitrate, especially with heavy nitrogen fertilization and unbalanced growing conditions. Boiling or blanching and discarding the water can lower the amount.

Nitrate deserves special attention for infant foods. It is reasonable to avoid large quantities of home-grown leafy amaranth with unknown nitrate levels in foods for very young children.

### Vitamin K and medication

People taking warfarin who eat large amounts of leafy amaranth should keep vitamin K intake consistent and discuss major dietary changes with their healthcare provider.

### Heavy metals

Amaranthus species can accumulate environmental contaminants such as cadmium. A meta-analysis found a notable potential for leaves to accumulate cadmium in contaminated soils. Do not grow edible amaranth in contaminated fill, beside polluted drainage, or where industrial wastewater may affect the soil.

## 7. Growing conditions {#growing-conditions}

Amaranth can grow in varied conditions, but drought tolerance does not mean it will produce a large crop without water.

| Factor | Suggested condition |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | About 21–29°C for leaf amaranth |
| Germination | Preferably in soil above 20°C |
| Light | Full sun; partial shade can help in extreme heat |
| Soil | Fertile, well-drained loam |
| pH | About 5.5–7.0 for leaf amaranth |
| Water | Keep moisture even |
| Drought | Tolerated, but yield declines |
| Waterlogging | Causes root oxygen shortage and risk of rot |
| Frost | Not tolerated |
| Fertilizer | Moderate; use care with nitrogen |

These values are based mainly on the University of Florida leaf-amaranth guide. Recommendations for grain amaranth differ somewhat; Ontario guidance, for example, gives a target soil pH of 6.5–7.5.

### Why does amaranth tolerate heat?

C4 photosynthesis helps amaranth maintain efficient carbon fixation at high temperatures. Its water-use efficiency can be better than that of many C3 crops, which is useful in sunny environments with high evaporation.

There are limits: prolonged water stress reduces leaf area and growth and may trigger premature flowering. Saline soil also reduces yield, and varieties differ.

## 8. Growing amaranth in Thailand {#thailand}

Thailand is naturally well suited to amaranth. Leaf production is simpler and faster than growing a crop to mature seed.

### 8.1. Thai names

Thai names include ผักโขม and ผักขม (phak khom).

| Thai name | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| ผักโขม | General name for edible amaranths |
| ผักโขมจีน | Chinese leaf amaranth, often A. tricolor |
| ผักโขมสวน | Garden amaranth |
| ผักโขมแดง | Red amaranth |
| ผักโขมหนาม | Spiny amaranth |

Common names do not always identify a species precisely, so check the scientific name when buying seed. Thailand's food-plant list separately recognizes species including A. tricolor, A. viridis and A. caudatus.

### 8.2. Seasons

| Period | Growing conditions |
| --- | --- |
| November–February | Often easiest, with fewer heavy rains |
| March–May | Heat is stronger; irrigation matters |
| May–October | Rainy season; drainage and disease management are critical |

Around Bangkok and Samut Prakan, November–February is a promising period for initial trials. During the rainy season, wet soil is a greater risk than heat.

### 8.3. The rainy-season challenge: root aeration

Amaranth tolerates drought but not prolonged standing water. When soil is saturated, oxygen cannot reach roots, growth slows and root diseases become more common. Raised beds need a drainage route to a sufficiently low outlet; a ditch without an outlet will not solve flooding. If the site repeatedly floods, raise containers or troughs above flood level.

University of Florida guidance suggests beds as high as about 50 cm for rainy conditions. For a practical trial on wet ground, 30–50 cm is a reasonable starting range.

### 8.4. Hydroponics

Amaranth can be grown in managed hydroponic systems, including recirculating nutrient solutions and drip irrigation into a growing medium. These systems allow close control of water and nutrients and avoid contaminated soil.

In Thailand, nutrient solution can overheat, reducing dissolved oxygen and increasing root-disease risk. For a first trial, simple substrate growing is a more manageable starting point than a large NFT system. Ensure clean water, aeration and temperature control.

## 9. A practical Thailand trial {#thailand-trial}

A proposed 10 m² trial could compare varieties, yield and rainy-season risks without a large initial investment.

### Trial layout

| Area | Type | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 m² | Green A. tricolor | Leaf growth and yield |
| 5 m² | Red A. tricolor | Growth, taste and leaf colour |

### Growing sequence

1. **Site:** Choose a sunny place with good drainage and clean, fertile growing medium.
2. **Sowing:** Sow about 0.5 cm deep and thin plants to 15–20 cm apart. Sow more densely if harvesting young leaves in one cut.
3. **Early growth:** Keep the medium evenly moist and prevent rain from compacting the surface or waterlogging roots.
4. **First harvest:** Often around 25–35 days after sowing, depending on plant size. Young leaves and shoots can also be picked individually.
5. **Further harvests:** For cut-and-come-again harvest, leave enough leaves and growing points. Another cut may be possible in about two weeks.
6. **Record results:** Weigh each harvest and note growing time, irrigation, weather, pests and leaf quality.

A Thai trial comparing amaranth seedlings reported yields of about 0.28–0.60 kg per square metre in one short growing period, depending on species. At that rate, 10 m² would yield a calculated 2.8–6.0 kg for one harvest. This is an illustration, not a prediction for a particular garden. Variety, density, harvest age and weather will change the result; later cuts are not included.

A Thai comparison of eight accessions also found substantial differences in growth and nutrient composition; local accession AS 129 performed particularly well. This supports testing varieties adapted to local conditions.

## 10. Growing grain amaranth in Thailand {#thailand-grain}

Grain production requires the plant to grow, flower, set seed and mature. Many varieties take about 100–120 days or longer and grow 1–2.5 m tall. Main risks include rain, fungal disease, lodging and drying the crop properly. Harvest usually involves threshing, cleaning and careful drying.

Some amaranths are sensitive to day length, which can affect flowering in both tropical and northern regions.

### When to grow for seed

First test a planting schedule that allows the seed to mature during a relatively dry season. Around Bangkok, late-year sowings may be worth testing. The challenge is often drying clean seed for storage. Ontario guidance recommends cleaning and drying seed to about 8% moisture.

### Possible grain yield

Reported yields vary widely. A commonly cited range is about 500–2,000 kg per hectare, equivalent to roughly 50–200 g of dry seed per square metre. Higher yields have been reported in successful trials. Hand harvesting a small plot may be labour-intensive relative to the amount of grain.

## 11. Growing amaranth in Finland {#finland}

In Finland, growing for leaves and growing for mature seed are different goals.

### Leaf amaranth

Leaf production is a feasible experiment. In Central Finland, start seedlings indoors in May and transplant into warm soil after frost risk has passed, often in early or mid-June. Direct sowing is also possible once the soil has warmed. A warm site, tunnel or greenhouse improves the odds. Leaves can be harvested during the same summer, and the plant can grow in a large pot.

### Seed production is harder

Many grain varieties need 100–120 days. The Finnish summer can be too short, and cool early-season weather slows growth. Day-length sensitivity may also delay flowering in the north. An early variety that responds little to day length is important.

Near Luhanka, begin with 5–10 plants in a warm, sheltered spot rather than a large plot. Ontario guidance also emphasizes warm soil, a frost-free season and proper drying.

## 12. Pests and plant diseases {#pests}

Amaranth is sometimes described as almost trouble-free, which is an overstatement. Problems can include leaf-eating caterpillars, aphids, leaf beetles, damping-off and root or stem diseases.

In tropical conditions, high humidity and dense planting can worsen fungal disease. Repeatedly growing the same crop in one place can increase pest and disease pressure. Good drainage, airflow, healthy seed, removal of plant residues and crop rotation are practical priorities. Use pesticides only when the product is approved for that food crop and country.

## 13. Taste and use {#taste}

### Leaves

Young leaves are often mild, similar to spinach with a slightly earthy taste. Older leaves may become tougher, and red varieties can colour cooking water.

In Thailand, leaf amaranth can be stir-fried with garlic, added to soups and omelettes, or cooked with other vegetables. It can also be steamed. For high-oxalate leaves, boiling and discarding the water can reduce oxalate.

### Seeds

Seeds have a mild, nutty and slightly earthy flavour. They can be used in porridge, soups, vegetable patties, flour blends, or toasted and popped.

Cooked amaranth is often soft and slightly sticky as its small seeds and starch form a cohesive texture. For porridge, try one part seed to 2.5–3 parts water and simmer for about 20–30 minutes.

Amaranth flour has no gluten network, so baking usually requires other flours or binding ingredients. People with coeliac disease should choose a product labelled gluten-free because cross-contamination can occur during milling and packaging.

## 14. Commercial potential {#commercial}

Amaranth may have commercial potential, but opportunities vary by product.

| Product | Production difficulty | Possible market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh leaves | Low–moderate | Local food sales |
| Specialty red leaves | Low–moderate | Restaurants and specialty vegetables |
| Microgreens | Moderate | Short production cycle; strict hygiene needed |
| Dry seed | Moderate–high | Gluten-free and specialty foods |
| Flour | Moderate–high | Food processing |
| Amaranth oil | High | Higher-value specialty product |
| Squalene extraction | Very high | Industrial specialty ingredient |
| Natural red pigments | High | Food and colour development |

This is a qualitative assessment, not a comparison based on market prices or proven profitability.

### Microgreens as an intermediate option

Amaranth microgreens can be harvested at about 10–18 days and fit small growing systems. A 2026 Thai study comparing red shoots at 7, 10 and 14 days found that yield and plant-chemical properties varied with harvest age. This could suit small-scale specialty production, but hygiene is especially important for shoots eaten raw.

### The economics of squalene

Amaranth oil is interesting because of its squalene, but seed oil is only a few percent of seed weight. If one tonne of seed contained 5% oil and the oil contained 4.2% squalene, the theoretical squalene amount would be about 2.1 kg. Processing losses would reduce the recovered amount. This makes industrial squalene extraction much more complex than selling leaves or seed.

## 15. Amaranth as a future crop {#future}

Amaranth has several traits of interest for climate-adapted agriculture: C4 photosynthesis, drought tolerance, ability to grow in varied soils and versatile food uses. Its genetic diversity may help breeders develop improved varieties.

Current varieties differ in flowering time, yield, heat tolerance, salt tolerance and nutrient composition. Breeding deserves as much attention as nutrient research; amaranth has not reached the same level of crop-development as wheat or rice.

Useful goals include earlier-maturing grain varieties, leaf varieties with better tolerance of wet conditions, lower-oxalate food crops, and lines with higher oil or betalain content.

Amaranth is an interesting underused food-crop group and a reasonable subject for cultivation trials in Thailand. Its value lies in the combination of nutritional, biological and agricultural traits rather than a single miracle compound.

### Further questions to investigate

Useful questions include which Thai varieties produce the best leaf yield and quality, how growing conditions affect oxalates and pigments, and whether raised beds or hydroponics can provide a consistent year-round crop. These are practical research topics; amaranth should not be treated as a miracle cure.

Key references used in this report include USDA FoodData Central, the [University of Florida growing guide](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/HS1407), [Ontario's grain amaranth guide](https://www.omafra.gov.on.ca/CropOp/en/field_grain/spec_grains/gama.html), [He and Corke, “Oil and squalene in amaranthus grain and leaf” (2003)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14690373/) and local Thai cultivation studies.
